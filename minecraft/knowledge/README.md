## Egypt (Old Kingdom)

<a id="pyramid_construction"></a>
### Pyramid construction: ramps, sledges and copper tools

*Giza, Dahshur, Saqqara - c. 2600-2500 BCE - construction*

The Giza pyramids were raised with inclined ramps, wooden sledges, levers and copper tools, by organised crews of paid labourers rather than slaves. Khufu's pyramid used about 2.3 million blocks averaging 2.5 tonnes, placed over roughly twenty years.

**How it works.** Limestone was quarried on site with copper chisels and dolerite pounders; fine Tura limestone casing and Aswan granite came by boat on the Nile flood. Blocks were dragged on sledges over wetted sand (halving friction, as the Djehutihotep tomb painting shows), hauled up ramps, and levered into place. Surveying used plumb bobs, set squares and water-levelled trenches, giving the base a level within about 2 cm.

**Why it matters.** Shows state logistics at scale: the Wadi al-Jarf papyri (Merer's diary, c. 2560 BCE) record a boat crew ferrying Tura limestone to Giza, with rations and shifts. Ramp form (straight, zigzag, spiral or internal) is still debated.

**In the games.** Origins: the Giza plateau; the pyramids are climbable and the interiors are explorable.

**In Minecraft.** `/function ac:egypt/giza` (landmark); `/function ac:tech/pyramid_ramp` (model)

**Sources.** Lehner, The Complete Pyramids (1997); Tallet & Lehner, The Red Sea Scrolls (2021); Fall et al., 'Sliding friction on wet and dry sand', Physical Review Letters 112 (2014)

## Egypt and Mesopotamia

<a id="shaduf"></a>
### Shaduf

*Nile and Tigris-Euphrates valleys - attested c. 2000 BCE Mesopotamia; Egypt by the New Kingdom (c. 1500 BCE) - water lifting*

A counterweighted lever on a post with a bucket on one end: the oldest machine for lifting irrigation water. It is still in use today.

**How it works.** A pole pivots on a crossbar; a bucket hangs from the long arm, a clay or stone counterweight sits on the short arm. The worker pulls the bucket down into the river, and the counterweight does most of the lifting as it swings up. One person can raise roughly 2 to 3 cubic metres an hour by 1 to 2 metres; several shadufs in series lift higher.

**Why it matters.** First practical application of the lever to agriculture; allowed garden plots above the level of basin irrigation to be watered through the dry season.

**In the games.** Origins: shadufs line every irrigated bank in the Nile Delta and the Faiyum.

**In Minecraft.** `/function ac:egypt/nile` (landmark); `/function ac:tech/shaduf` (model)

**Sources.** Butzer, Early Hydraulic Civilization in Egypt (1976); Oleson, Greek and Roman Mechanical Water-Lifting Devices (1984)

## Egypt

<a id="nilometer"></a>
### Nilometer and basin irrigation

*Elephantine, Roda (Cairo), Edfu - Old Kingdom onward; Roda nilometer rebuilt 861 CE - hydraulics / measurement*

Graduated stairs, wells or columns that recorded the height of the annual Nile flood, from which the harvest and the year's tax rate were predicted. Basin irrigation trapped the flood behind earth dikes so silt settled and the soil soaked.

**How it works.** A channel from the river fills a stone-lined shaft; marks in cubits on the wall record the water level. About 16 cubits at Roda meant a good year, 12 meant famine, 20 meant destructive flooding. Basins were opened and drained in sequence down the valley as the flood receded.

**Why it matters.** Tied astronomy (the heliacal rising of Sirius announced the flood), measurement, taxation and agriculture into one state system for three thousand years.

**In the games.** Origins; the Roda nilometer of 861 belongs to Mirage's decade.

**In Minecraft.** `/function ac:egypt/nile` (landmark)

**Sources.** Willcocks, Egyptian Irrigation (1889); Said, The River Nile (1993)

<a id="papyrus_and_calendar"></a>
### Papyrus, the 365-day civil calendar and water clocks

*Nile Delta - 3rd millennium BCE onward - information / timekeeping*

Egypt supplied the ancient world's writing material and its most practical calendar. The civil year of 12 thirty-day months plus 5 epagomenal days ignored the leap day and so drifted, but it was simple and became the basis of the Julian and Gregorian calendars.

**How it works.** Papyrus pith was sliced into strips, laid in two crossed layers, pressed and dried; the plant's sap glued it. Hours were kept by shadow clocks and by water clocks (clepsydrae), conical vessels with graduated interior scales, the oldest surviving from Karnak c. 1400 BCE.

**Why it matters.** Standardised administration, taxation and record keeping; the Library of Alexandria's scrolls were papyrus.

**In the games.** Origins: the Library of Alexandria and the scroll trade.

**In Minecraft.** `/function ac:egypt/alexandria` (landmark)

**Sources.** Parkinson & Quirke, Papyrus (1995); Clagett, Ancient Egyptian Science, vol. 2 (1995)

## Egypt (New Kingdom)

<a id="obelisk_quarrying"></a>
### Obelisk quarrying and raising

*Aswan granite quarries, Karnak - c. 1500-1200 BCE - stone working / transport*

Single-piece granite monoliths of up to 30 metres and 300 tonnes were pounded out of the Aswan bedrock, floated downriver on barges, and tipped upright into a sand-filled pit.

**How it works.** Workers pounded trenches around the shaft with balls of dolerite (harder than granite), undercut it, and levered it free. The Unfinished Obelisk at Aswan (abandoned when it cracked) shows the method; Hatshepsut's reliefs at Deir el-Bahari show a pair carried on one huge barge. Raising probably used an embankment and a sand-filled funnel pit that was slowly emptied so the shaft rotated onto its base.

**Why it matters.** The heaviest objects moved by any premodern society without wheels or pulleys; Rome later shipped over a dozen to Italy.

**In the games.** Origins: obelisks stand before the temples at Memphis and Karnak; Mirage's Baghdad imported none, but Rome and Constantinople (Revelations hippodrome) did.

**In Minecraft.** `/function ac:egypt/memphis` (landmark); `/function ac:tech/obelisk_quarry` (model)

**Sources.** Engelbach, The Problem of the Obelisks (1923); Arnold, Building in Egypt (1991)

## Sumer, Babylon

<a id="ziggurat_mudbrick"></a>
### Ziggurats and fired brick

*Ur, Uruk, Babylon - Ur ziggurat c. 2100 BCE - construction / materials*

Stepped temple platforms of sun-dried mud brick cased in kiln-fired brick set in bitumen, with weep holes to drain the core. The Ur ziggurat's lowest stage measured 64 by 46 metres.

**How it works.** Sun-dried brick is cheap but dissolves in rain; a skin of fired brick bonded with bitumen (from natural seeps) sealed the outside. Reed mats and ropes laid between courses stopped the core from slumping. Three converging stairs led to the shrine on top.

**Why it matters.** Mesopotamia invented large-scale brick architecture, the arch and the true vault in brick, and glazed brick (Ishtar Gate, 575 BCE).

**In the games.** Mirage: Baghdad's mud-brick walls continued the same tradition, and Babylon's ruins lie a day's ride south.

**In Minecraft.** `/function ac:tech/ziggurat` (model)

**Sources.** Woolley, Ur Excavations V: The Ziggurat (1939); Oates, Babylon (1986)

## Mesopotamia, Eurasian steppe, Egypt

<a id="wheel_and_chariot"></a>
### The wheel, the potter's wheel and the spoked-wheel chariot

*Uruk; Sintashta (Urals); Hyksos Egypt - potter's wheel c. 3500 BCE; wheeled vehicles c. 3500-3300 BCE; spoked chariots c. 2000 BCE - transport*

Solid disc wheels on carts appear almost simultaneously across Eurasia around 3500 BCE; the light spoked-wheel chariot, from the steppe c. 2000 BCE, reached Egypt with the Hyksos and became the Bronze Age weapons platform.

**How it works.** Early wheels were three-plank discs pinned together, turning on a fixed axle. Spokes, bent-wood felloes and a rotating axle with lubricated hubs cut the weight to under 35 kg, light enough for two horses to carry an archer at speed.

**Why it matters.** Transformed agriculture (carts), warfare (chariots) and crafts (the potter's wheel is the first machine to store rotary energy in a flywheel).

**In the games.** Origins: chariot racing at the Hippodrome of Alexandria and Cyrene.

**In Minecraft.** `/function ac:tech/chariot` (model)

**Sources.** Anthony, The Horse, the Wheel, and Language (2007); Littauer & Crouwel, Wheeled Vehicles and Ridden Animals in the Ancient Near East (1979)

## Sumer

<a id="cuneiform"></a>
### Cuneiform, clay tokens and accounting

*Uruk - tokens from 8000 BCE; proto-cuneiform c. 3300 BCE - information*

Writing began as accounting. Clay tokens standing for goods were sealed in clay envelopes, then impressed on the outside, then drawn on tablets; wedge-shaped (cuneiform) signs pressed with a reed stylus followed.

**How it works.** A reed stylus pressed into damp clay leaves triangular marks; combinations encode syllables and words. Tablets were sun-dried (reusable) or fired (permanent). The sexagesimal (base 60) place-value system gave us 60-minute hours and 360-degree circles.

**Why it matters.** Enabled contracts, law codes (Hammurabi, c. 1750 BCE), astronomy and the first libraries (Ashurbanipal at Nineveh).

**In the games.** Mirage: Abbasid scholars in the House of Wisdom inherited Babylonian astronomy through Greek and Persian intermediaries.

**In Minecraft.** `/function ac:baghdad/house_of_wisdom` (landmark)

**Sources.** Schmandt-Besserat, How Writing Came About (1996); Robson, Mathematics in Ancient Iraq (2008)

## Persia (Achaemenid and earlier)

<a id="qanat"></a>
### Qanat

*Iranian plateau, later Oman, North Africa, Spain - first millennium BCE - hydraulics*

A gently sloping underground tunnel that taps an aquifer at the foot of the mountains and carries water by gravity for kilometres to fields and towns, without pumps and with almost no evaporation.

**How it works.** Surveyors sink a mother well to find the water table, then dig a line of vertical shafts every 20 to 30 metres for spoil removal and ventilation; the tunnel is driven between them on a gradient of about 1 in 1000. The water surfaces where the tunnel meets the ground.

**Why it matters.** Made settled life possible across arid Iran and spread with Persian and Islamic rule as far as Madrid and the Sahara; Gonabad's qanat still runs after about 2,700 years.

**In the games.** Mirage: Baghdad's water came largely from canals off the Euphrates, but qanats fed Persian cities of the same caliphate.

**In Minecraft.** `/function ac:tech/qanat` (model)

**Sources.** English, 'The Origin and Spread of Qanats in the Old World', Proceedings of the American Philosophical Society 112 (1968); Goblot, Les qanats (1979)

## Persia / Islamic world

<a id="panemone_windmill"></a>
### Vertical-axis windmill (panemone)

*Sistan (eastern Iran, western Afghanistan) - attested 9th century CE (al-Masudi); possibly 7th century - power*

The first windmills turned on a vertical axis: a drum of reed-mat sails inside a walled enclosure with an opening that admits the wind to one side only. They ground grain and pumped water in the Sistan 'wind of 120 days'.

**How it works.** Wind enters through a slot and pushes the exposed sails; the walls shield the returning sails. The vertical shaft drives a millstone directly, no gearing needed. Efficiency is low but the construction is simple.

**Why it matters.** First conversion of wind into rotary power for work on land; the horizontal-axis European windmill is a separate 12th-century invention.

**In the games.** Mirage: these mills stood on the eastern edge of the Abbasid caliphate in Basim's lifetime.

**In Minecraft.** `/function ac:tech/panemone` (model)

**Sources.** al-Hassan & Hill, Islamic Technology: An Illustrated History (1986); Hill, A History of Engineering in Classical and Medieval Times (1984)

## Hellenistic Greece / Ptolemaic Egypt

<a id="archimedes_screw"></a>
### Archimedes' screw

*Alexandria, Syracuse - 3rd century BCE - water lifting*

A helical blade inside an inclined tube; turning it lifts water in pockets up the spiral. Used to drain mines and ships' bilges and to irrigate the Nile Delta, where Diodorus says Archimedes saw it (or devised it) in Egypt.

**How it works.** The screw is inclined at about 30 to 45 degrees with its lower end in the water. Each turn traps a pocket of water between the blade and the casing and carries it one pitch higher. Workers turned it by treading on the outside of the casing.

**Why it matters.** Still used in sewage works and for fish-friendly hydropower; a clear example of rotary motion applied to lifting.

**In the games.** Origins: Alexandria's Mouseion is where Archimedes studied; screws water the Delta fields.

**In Minecraft.** `/function ac:egypt/alexandria` (landmark); `/function ac:tech/archimedes_screw` (model)

**Sources.** Oleson, Greek and Roman Mechanical Water-Lifting Devices (1984); Rorres, 'The Turn of the Screw', Journal of Hydraulic Engineering 126 (2000)

## Hellenistic Greece

<a id="antikythera_mechanism"></a>
### Antikythera mechanism

*probably Rhodes or Corinth; found in a wreck off Antikythera - c. 150-60 BCE - astronomy / gearing*

A hand-cranked bronze geared calculator that displayed the positions of the Sun, Moon and probably the five planets, the Moon's phase, eclipse predictions (Saros cycle) and the dates of the Olympic Games. At least 30 bronze gears survive.

**How it works.** Turning a crank advances a 365-day calendar dial; gear trains with precise tooth counts reproduce astronomical ratios, including a pin-and-slot device that models the Moon's varying speed (its anomaly). The back dials spiral through the 19-year Metonic and 223-month Saros cycles.

**Why it matters.** Nothing of comparable mechanical complexity survives until the medieval astronomical clocks 1,400 years later; it proves Greek gear cutting and astronomical theory were far ahead of the surviving literary record.

**In the games.** Odyssey and Origins are set exactly in its era; Rhodes and Alexandria were centres of such work.

**In Minecraft.** `/function ac:tech/antikythera` (model)

**Sources.** Freeth et al., 'Decoding the ancient Greek astronomical calculator', Nature 444 (2006); Jones, A Portable Cosmos (2017)

## Greece

<a id="trireme"></a>
### The trireme

*Athens (Piraeus), Corinth - c. 600-300 BCE - naval*

A 37-metre oared warship rowed by 170 men on three levels, with a bronze ram at the waterline. Athens kept 200 or more; they won at Salamis in 480 BCE and were the backbone of the Delian League.

**How it works.** Light pine hull built shell-first with mortise-and-tenon planking; the outrigger let a third bank of rowers sit above and outboard of the other two. At about 8 knots sprint speed the ram broke an enemy's planking. Sails were for cruising only and were left ashore before battle.

**Why it matters.** Expensive to build and crew, so sea power demanded democratic mobilisation of the citizen rowers; the ship's archaeology was tested by the 1987 reconstruction Olympias.

**In the games.** Odyssey: the Adrestia and every fleet battle.

**In Minecraft.** `/function ac:greece/trireme` (landmark)

**Sources.** Morrison, Coates & Rankov, The Athenian Trireme (2nd ed., 2000)

## Greece (Syracuse, Macedon), Rome

<a id="torsion_catapult"></a>
### Torsion catapults

*Syracuse, Alexandria, Rhodes - gastraphetes c. 399 BCE; torsion engines from c. 340 BCE - military*

The first artillery. The gastraphetes was a giant crossbow cocked by the belly; torsion engines replaced the bow with two bundles of twisted sinew or hair that stored far more energy, throwing bolts or stones of up to 80 kg.

**How it works.** Each arm is thrust through a skein of sinew rope wound tight in a frame; winding the bowstring back twists the skeins further. A trigger releases the arms. Philo of Byzantium and Hero record a design formula: the spring-hole diameter in dactyls is 1.1 times the cube root of 100 times the shot weight in minas.

**Why it matters.** Engineering by calculation: the formula let any arsenal scale an engine to a target shot weight, an early instance of design rules derived from experiment.

**In the games.** Odyssey siege battles; Origins' Roman ballistae on the walls of Alexandria.

**In Minecraft.** `/function ac:tech/catapult` (model)

**Sources.** Marsden, Greek and Roman Artillery: Historical Development (1969); Rihll, The Catapult: A History (2007)

## Roman Egypt

<a id="hero_of_alexandria"></a>
### Hero of Alexandria: the aeolipile and automata

*Alexandria - 1st century CE - mechanics / pneumatics*

Hero described the aeolipile, a steam-driven reaction sphere, plus a coin-operated holy-water dispenser, automatic temple doors opened by heated air, a wind-powered organ and programmable puppet theatres driven by weights and knotted cords.

**How it works.** In the aeolipile, water boils in a cauldron; steam rises through pipes into a pivoted sphere and exits through two bent nozzles, spinning it by reaction. The automata used expanding air, siphons and falling weights; a string wound around a drum with pegs set the sequence of motions.

**Why it matters.** No one turned the aeolipile into a working engine (no need, with cheap labour, and no metallurgy for pressure), but it shows that the principles of steam and programmed control were understood in antiquity.

**In the games.** Origins: Alexandria's Mouseion and the engineering school where Hero and Ctesibius taught.

**In Minecraft.** `/function ac:egypt/alexandria` (landmark); `/function ac:tech/aeolipile` (model)

**Sources.** Hero, Pneumatica (trans. Woodcroft, 1851); Landels, Engineering in the Ancient World (1978)

## Ptolemaic Egypt

<a id="pharos"></a>
### The Pharos of Alexandria

*Alexandria - completed c. 280 BCE - construction / navigation*

A lighthouse of about 100 metres or more in three tiers (square, octagonal, round) on the island of Pharos, built under Ptolemy I and II by Sostratus of Cnidus. It stood until earthquakes in the 14th century.

**How it works.** A fire at the top, possibly backed by a polished bronze mirror, marked the harbour entrance at night; an internal ramp let donkeys carry fuel up. The lower tier was large enough to hold storerooms and quarters.

**Why it matters.** One of the Seven Wonders and the model for every lighthouse since; its name became the word for lighthouse in Romance languages (faro, phare).

**In the games.** Origins: Bayek climbs it.

**In Minecraft.** `/function ac:egypt/alexandria` (landmark)

**Sources.** Clayton & Price, The Seven Wonders of the Ancient World (1988); Empereur, Alexandria Rediscovered (1998)

## Classical Greece

<a id="greek_temple_engineering"></a>
### Greek temple engineering and optical refinements

*Athens (Parthenon), Olympia, Delphi - Parthenon 447-432 BCE - construction*

Post-and-lintel marble temples assembled from drums and blocks joined with iron clamps and dowels set in lead, without mortar. The Parthenon's apparently straight lines are all subtly curved to correct the eye.

**How it works.** Column drums were lifted with cranes using lewis holes and rope grooves, then ground together by rotation on a wooden pin. The stylobate rises about 6 cm at the centre of each side, columns lean inward and swell (entasis), corner columns are thicker: optical refinements that make the building look rigid and straight.

**Why it matters.** The precision (joints invisible to the naked eye) and the geometry became the vocabulary of Western architecture.

**In the games.** Odyssey: the Parthenon is being completed as the game begins.

**In Minecraft.** `/function ac:greece/athens` (landmark)

**Sources.** Coulton, Ancient Greek Architects at Work (1977); Korres, From Pentelicon to the Parthenon (1995)

## Rome

<a id="roman_concrete"></a>
### Roman concrete (opus caementicium)

*Italy and the whole empire; marine harbours at Caesarea and Baiae - c. 200 BCE onward; Pantheon 126 CE - materials*

Lime mixed with volcanic ash (pozzolana) and rubble aggregate set hard even under water and grew stronger with age. It made possible the Pantheon's 43-metre unreinforced dome, still the world's largest.

**How it works.** Quicklime, pozzolana and water react to form calcium-aluminium-silicate hydrates that bind the aggregate; in seawater the mix grows aluminous tobermorite crystals that heal cracks. Builders graded the aggregate, using heavy basalt at the base of the Pantheon dome and light pumice near the oculus.

**Why it matters.** Freed architecture from post-and-lintel limits: vaults, domes, harbours and aqueduct cores. The recipe was lost after Rome and only matched by Portland cement in the 19th century.

**In the games.** Brotherhood: the Pantheon and Colosseum; Valhalla's Lunden is built among Roman concrete ruins.

**In Minecraft.** `/function ac:italy/rome` (landmark)

**Sources.** Jackson et al., 'Phillipsite and Al-tobermorite mineral cements produced through low-temperature water-rock reactions in Roman marine concrete', American Mineralogist 102 (2017); Lancaster, Concrete Vaulted Construction in Imperial Rome (2005)

<a id="aqueduct"></a>
### Aqueducts

*Rome (11 aqueducts), Nimes (Pont du Gard), Segovia - Aqua Appia 312 BCE to Aqua Alexandrina 226 CE - hydraulics*

Gravity-fed channels that brought spring water tens of kilometres to cities, mostly underground, with bridges and arcades only where valleys demanded. Rome's eleven lines delivered on the order of a million cubic metres a day.

**How it works.** Surveyors (using the chorobates water level and the groma) set gradients as shallow as 1 in 3000; the Pont du Gard carries its channel 50 km on a fall of 17 metres. Where a valley was too deep for arches, an inverted siphon of lead pipes carried water down and up again under pressure. In the city a distribution tank (castellum) split flow to baths, fountains and private houses.

**Why it matters.** Public health, baths and fountains for a city of a million; Frontinus' treatise on the water supply (97 CE) is the first engineering management manual.

**In the games.** Brotherhood: Ezio rebuilds Rome's aqueduct; Valhalla's Lunden still uses Roman channels.

**In Minecraft.** `/function ac:tech/aqueduct` (model)

**Sources.** Hodge, Roman Aqueducts and Water Supply (1992); Frontinus, De aquaeductu urbis Romae

<a id="roman_roads"></a>
### Roman roads

*Via Appia (312 BCE) and about 80,000 km of paved roads empire-wide - 312 BCE onward - transport / surveying*

Engineered roads built in layers on a surveyed straight alignment, drained by ditches and cambered, with milestones, bridges and relay stations for the imperial post.

**How it works.** A trench was dug to firm ground and filled with the statumen (large stones), rudus (rubble in lime), nucleus (gravel concrete) and summum dorsum (fitted polygonal basalt slabs). Agrimensores laid the line with the groma (a cross of plumb lines for right angles) and sighting poles.

**Why it matters.** Armies marched 30 km a day and news travelled 80 km a day; many of the routes are still roads.

**In the games.** Brotherhood's Roman countryside; Valhalla's England still used the Roman network (Watling Street, Ermine Street).

**In Minecraft.** `/function ac:italy/rome` (landmark); `/function ac:tech/roman_road` (model)

**Sources.** Chevallier, Roman Roads (1976); Laurence, The Roads of Roman Italy (1999)

<a id="hypocaust"></a>
### Hypocaust heating and the public bath

*empire-wide; Baths of Caracalla (216 CE) - 1st century BCE onward - building services*

Underfloor heating: the floor sits on short brick piers, and hot gases from a furnace (praefurnium) circulate beneath it and up through hollow box tiles (tubuli) in the walls before leaving by flues in the roof.

**How it works.** Slaves stoked a wood furnace at one end; the floor of the hot room (caldarium) nearest the furnace reached 50 degrees C and bathers wore sandals. Rooms were sequenced warm to hot to cold (tepidarium, caldarium, frigidarium) and water was heated in bronze boilers over the same fire.

**Why it matters.** The baths were the social centre of every Roman town; the Baths of Caracalla held 1,600 bathers and consumed about 10 tonnes of wood a day.

**In the games.** Brotherhood: the Baths of Diocletian and Trajan in Rome.

<a id="barbegal_watermill"></a>
### Watermills and the Barbegal cascade

*Barbegal near Arles, Gaul - vertical watermill described by Vitruvius 1st century BCE; Barbegal early 2nd century CE - power*

The vertical overshot waterwheel geared to a horizontal millstone is the first industrial prime mover. At Barbegal an aqueduct fed sixteen wheels in two parallel cascades down a hillside, grinding grain for the region.

**How it works.** Water fills buckets on the wheel's rim and its weight turns the wheel (overshot wheels reach 60-70 percent efficiency). A right-angle gear (the Vitruvian gear train) turns the vertical shaft of the runner stone. Each Barbegal wheel passed its tailwater to the next.

**Why it matters.** Shows Rome did build mechanised production when fuel and water allowed; medieval Europe later built a watermill in almost every village (over 5,600 in Domesday Book, 1086).

**In the games.** Valhalla: Norse and Saxon mills on English rivers; Rogue and III have the colonial sawmill.

## Etruscan and Roman

<a id="arch_vault_dome"></a>
### The arch, the vault and the dome

*Italy - true arch in Etruria 4th century BCE; Roman vaults from 2nd century BCE - construction*

Wedge-shaped voussoirs locked by a keystone turn a span into pure compression, which stone and brick carry well. Extruded, the arch becomes a barrel vault; rotated, a dome; two vaults crossing make a groin vault that needs support only at the corners.

**How it works.** The arch is built on a timber centering, voussoirs added from both springers until the keystone closes it; only then does it carry load. Its thrust pushes outward, so abutments or buttresses are needed. Roman engineers stacked arcades (Colosseum, Pont du Gard) and used brick ribs in concrete vaults.

**Why it matters.** The structural idea behind every bridge, aqueduct, basilica, mosque and cathedral of the next 1,800 years.

**In the games.** Brotherhood's Colosseum; Revelations' Hagia Sophia (dome on pendentives); Unity's Notre-Dame (pointed arches).

## Greece and Rome

<a id="treadwheel_crane"></a>
### Treadwheel cranes and the polyspastos

*empire-wide; relief in the tomb of the Haterii, Rome - pulleys 6th century BCE Greece; Roman treadwheel crane 1st century BCE - lifting*

Compound pulleys (polyspastos) and a human-powered treadwheel let a few men lift stones of several tonnes; Vitruvius describes the standard three-pulley crane (trispastos) and larger variants.

**How it works.** A rope runs over multiple sheaves so that the load is shared: with five pulleys, a man exerting 50 kg lifts 250 kg less friction. A treadwheel of 4 to 5 metres diameter turned by men walking inside gives a further 1:20 or so, so a crane could raise about 6 tonnes with four men. Lewis irons gripped the block from a dovetailed hole.

**Why it matters.** Replaced Egyptian-style ramps and mass labour with mechanical advantage; medieval cathedrals reused the same treadwheel design.

**In the games.** Brotherhood and Unity: building sites in Rome and Paris show treadwheel cranes.

## Assyria, Greece, Rome

<a id="siege_engineering"></a>
### Siege towers, rams and circumvallation

*Lachish (701 BCE), Tyre (332 BCE), Masada (73 CE), Alesia (52 BCE) - 9th century BCE onward - military*

Assyrian reliefs show wheeled siege towers with rams by 865 BCE; Alexander's engineers built 50-metre towers (helepolis) and Rome perfected the circumvallation, a wall around the besieged city to starve it.

**How it works.** A tower on wheels, sheathed in hides against fire, is pushed up a ramp to the wall so that archers on top clear the parapet and a drawbridge drops onto it. Rams swing from chains inside a roofed shed (testudo). Mines dug under walls were propped with timber, then fired so the wall collapsed.

**Why it matters.** Fortification and siegecraft drove each other for three millennia until gunpowder artillery ended the tall curtain wall.

## Han China

<a id="paper"></a>
### Paper

*China; spread via Samarkand (751) and Baghdad (794) to Europe (Xativa, 1150) - earliest fragments 2nd century BCE; Cai Lun's improved process 105 CE - information / materials*

Paper made from macerated plant fibre (hemp, mulberry bark, rags) replaced bamboo strips and silk in China and papyrus and parchment in the West, cutting the cost of writing by an order of magnitude.

**How it works.** Fibres are soaked, beaten to a pulp, suspended in water and lifted out on a fine screen; the sheet drains, is pressed and dried, and the fibres bond by hydrogen bonding alone. Sizing with starch or gelatin stops ink spreading.

**Why it matters.** Precondition for printing, cheap books, paper money (Song China) and bureaucracy; the Abbasids adopted it for all state records within fifty years of meeting it.

**In the games.** Mirage: Baghdad's paper market (Suq al-Warraqin) is where Basim's story begins; Chronicles: China.

### Zhang Heng's seismoscope and Chinese instruments

*Luoyang - 132 CE - instruments*

Zhang Heng's bronze urn dropped a ball from one of eight dragon mouths into a toad below, indicating the direction of a distant earthquake. Han engineers also built water-driven armillary spheres and the south-pointing chariot (a differential-gear direction keeper).

**How it works.** Probably an inverted pendulum inside the urn that swung toward the first ground motion and tripped a lever releasing the ball on that side. The south-pointing chariot used a differential gear so that the figure's arm turned opposite to the chariot's turn and kept pointing the same way.

## China (Han to Song)

<a id="compass"></a>
### The magnetic compass

*China; in use by Chinese sailors by 1100, Mediterranean by 1190 - lodestone south-pointer by the 1st century CE; magnetised needle described by Shen Kuo in 1088 - navigation*

A lodestone spoon pivoted on a bronze plate was a Han divination tool; by the Song, iron needles magnetised by stroking with lodestone and floated in water or hung on silk gave sailors a bearing in overcast weather.

**How it works.** A magnetised needle aligns with the Earth's field. Shen Kuo noted it points slightly east of true south (declination). Mariners combined it with a wind rose to steer courses, and with the portolan chart in Europe from the 13th century.

**Why it matters.** Allowed year-round sailing out of sight of land and underpinned the Indian Ocean trade and the later European voyages.

## Tang and Song China

<a id="gunpowder"></a>
### Gunpowder and early firearms

*China; Islamic world by 1240s; Europe by 1267 - first formula 9th century; Wujing Zongyao 1044; fire lance 1132; bronze hand cannon 1288 - military / chemistry*

Daoist alchemists seeking an elixir mixed saltpetre, sulphur and charcoal and found it burned violently. It became fire arrows, bombs, the fire lance (a bamboo tube spitting flame and shrapnel) and by the late 13th century the true gun.

**How it works.** Saltpetre (potassium nitrate) supplies oxygen so the mix burns without air; raising its share from about 50 percent (incendiary) to 75 percent (explosive) made a propellant. Corning the powder into grains, a 15th-century European step, made it burn evenly in a gun barrel.

**Why it matters.** Ended the dominance of the stone castle and the armoured horseman, and spread along the Mongol routes to the whole of Eurasia within a century.

**In the games.** Revelations: Ottoman bombards; Chronicles China 1526; Black Flag and III are gunpowder worlds.

## China, Korea, Europe

<a id="printing"></a>
### Woodblock printing, movable type and the press

*Dunhuang (Diamond Sutra 868), Korea (Jikji 1377), Mainz (Gutenberg c. 1450) - 7th century to 1450 - information*

Chinese printers carved whole pages in wood; Bi Sheng made ceramic movable type in the 1040s and Korea cast bronze type by 1234. Gutenberg's contribution was the system: a hand mould for casting uniform metal type, oil-based ink and a screw press.

**How it works.** Type is cast in an adjustable mould from a lead-tin-antimony alloy that fills the matrix cleanly. Pages are composed in a forme, inked with leather balls, and the screw press forces damp paper onto the type. A trained shop printed about 250 sheets an hour.

**Why it matters.** In fifty years Europe printed more books than scribes had copied in a thousand; the standardised text was the medium of the Reformation and the scientific revolution.

## China (Zhou to Han)

<a id="cast_iron_blast_furnace"></a>
### Chinese cast iron and the water-powered blast furnace

*Henan and Hebei - cast iron by 5th century BCE; Du Shi's water-powered bellows 31 CE - metallurgy*

China cast iron 1,700 years before Europe. Tall furnaces with powerful bellows reached the 1,150 degrees C needed to melt iron with its carbon, and the liquid was poured into moulds for ploughshares, pots and tools, or decarburised into steel.

**How it works.** Layers of charcoal and ore burn in a shaft furnace while bellows drive air through tuyeres; phosphorus-rich ores and fluxes lowered the melting point. Du Shi used a waterwheel to drive the bellows; the Han state ran 48 iron monopolies. Fining (stirring molten iron in air) and co-fusion with wrought iron gave steel.

**Why it matters.** Mass-produced iron tools raised Chinese farm productivity; Europe's bloomery iron could only be hammered, not cast, until about 1400.

**In the games.** Chronicles: China; the Great Wall's Ming phase used iron tools and cast-iron cannon.

## Sui and Song China

<a id="pound_lock_grand_canal"></a>
### The Grand Canal and the pound lock

*Grand Canal, Hangzhou to Beijing (1,800 km) - Grand Canal 605-609 CE; pound lock by Qiao Weiyue 984 CE - hydraulics / transport*

The world's longest canal linked the Yangtze rice basket to the northern capitals. Qiao Weiyue's double-gate lock let barges change level without the flash locks' loss of water and cargo.

**How it works.** Two gates 75 metres apart enclose a chamber; a boat enters, the gate closes, and sluices fill or drain the chamber to the next level before the far gate opens. Summit sections were fed by reservoirs. Europe's first pound locks came in the Netherlands in 1373.

**Why it matters.** Grain tax moved by water at a fraction of land cost, holding the empire together; the lock is the key component of every later canal and of the Panama Canal.

## China (Qin to Ming)

<a id="great_wall"></a>
### The Great Wall: rammed earth to Ming brick

*northern frontier - Qin unification 221 BCE; Ming brick walls 1368-1644 - construction / military*

Early walls were rammed earth (hangtu) packed in timber forms, layer by layer; the Ming rebuilt thousands of kilometres in fired brick and stone with battlements, beacon towers and garrison forts.

**How it works.** Rammed earth: moist loess is tamped in 10 cm layers between boards, reaching the strength of soft rock. Ming walls: a rubble core faced with brick set in lime mortar, often mixed with sticky-rice soup whose amylopectin makes the mortar tougher and water resistant. Beacon towers signalled with smoke by day and fire by night, with the number of fires coding the enemy's strength.

**Why it matters.** Not one wall but a system of frontiers; the sticky-rice mortar is an early composite material.

## South India and Sri Lanka

<a id="wootz_steel"></a>
### Wootz crucible steel and Damascus blades

*Deccan, Tamil Nadu; traded to Damascus and Persia - c. 300 BCE to 1700 CE - metallurgy*

Wrought iron, charcoal and plant matter sealed in clay crucibles and fired for days produced ingots of high-carbon (1.5 percent) steel. Forged slowly, they gave the watered 'Damascus' pattern and a blade that held an edge like nothing in Europe.

**How it works.** In the crucible the iron absorbs carbon and partly melts; slow cooling grows networks of cementite. Trace vanadium and molybdenum in specific Indian ores help the carbide bands form during forging at low red heat. Sri Lankan smelters used monsoon-wind furnaces on hillsides.

**Why it matters.** First industrial steel by melting; the pattern was never reproduced in Europe until 19th-century metallurgists analysed it.

## Gupta India

<a id="iron_pillar_delhi"></a>
### The Iron Pillar of Delhi

*Delhi (Qutb complex) - c. 400 CE - metallurgy*

A 7.2-metre, 6-tonne pillar of wrought iron forged from many blooms, standing in the open for 1,600 years with almost no rust.

**How it works.** The iron is high in phosphorus (about 0.25 percent) and low in sulphur; in Delhi's dry climate a thin, adherent layer of crystalline iron hydrogen phosphate forms and seals the surface. **Why it matters.** Evidence of Indian forge-welding skill and the earliest known passivated iron surface.

Monumental wells with flights of steps descending many storeys to the water table, so that water could be reached whatever the season and the shaded lower levels stayed cool.

## Gupta and post-Gupta India

<a id="indian_numerals"></a>
### Indian numerals and zero

*India; transmitted through Baghdad (al-Khwarizmi, c. 825) to Europe (Fibonacci, 1202) - place-value decimal by 5th century CE; Brahmagupta's rules for zero 628 CE - mathematics*

Ten digits with place value and a sign for zero, treated by Brahmagupta as a number with arithmetic rules. Al-Khwarizmi's Baghdad treatise carried the system west, where the numerals were called Arabic.

**How it works.** Each position is a power of ten; zero holds an empty place and also behaves as a number (a minus a equals zero). Calculation on a dust board (hence 'algorithm', from al-Khwarizmi's name) replaced the abacus.

**Why it matters.** Made long division, algebra and double-entry bookkeeping practical; the foundation of all later quantitative science.

## Hellenistic Greece, perfected in the Islamic world

<a id="astrolabe"></a>
### The astrolabe

*Alexandria; Baghdad, Toledo, Isfahan - theory from Hipparchus (2nd century BCE); oldest surviving instruments 9th-10th century CE - astronomy / navigation*

A flat model of the sky: a rotating star map (rete) over a plate engraved with the horizon and altitude lines for one latitude. With a sighting of one star's altitude it gives the time, the direction of Mecca, sunrise, and the rising sign, and it solves spherical-astronomy problems by rotation.

**How it works.** The celestial sphere is projected stereographically onto the plane of the equator, so circles stay circles. Rotating the rete until the sighted star's pointer sits on the measured altitude circle sets the whole sky to the current moment; the hour is read from the rim.

**Why it matters.** The portable computer of the medieval world, used by astronomers, muezzins, surveyors and (in simplified form) navigators until the sextant.

**In the games.** Mirage: astrolabes hang in the House of Wisdom; Revelations' Ottoman scholars used them.

## Artuqid state (Islamic world)

<a id="al_jazari"></a>
### Al-Jazari's machines: crank, camshaft and the elephant clock

*Diyarbakir, upper Mesopotamia - Book of Knowledge of Ingenious Mechanical Devices, 1206 - mechanics*

Al-Jazari described fifty machines: water-raising pumps using the first known crankshaft and a double-acting suction pump, segmental gears, camshaft-driven automata, a programmable musical boat, and the elephant water clock that combined Indian, Greek, Chinese and Arab elements.

**How it works.** The elephant clock: a perforated bowl floats in a water tank inside the elephant and sinks over half an hour; a string pulls a seesaw that releases a ball, which strikes a cymbal and drops into a serpent's mouth, resetting the bowl, while the scribe's pen marks the minutes. His reciprocating pump used a crank and connecting rod to turn rotary into linear motion, three centuries before European use.

**Why it matters.** The richest surviving record of medieval mechanical engineering; the crank-and-rod and the camshaft are the heart of every engine.

**In the games.** Mirage's Baghdad is three centuries earlier, but its engineers (the Banu Musa, 9th century) wrote the Book of Ingenious Devices that al-Jazari built on.

## Hellenistic and Islamic Middle East

<a id="noria_saqiya"></a>
### Noria and saqiya waterwheels

*Hama (Syria), Egypt, al-Andalus - saqiya in Ptolemaic Egypt 3rd century BCE; the Hama norias from Byzantine times - water lifting*

The noria is a river-driven wheel with pots on its rim that empty into an aqueduct at the top; the saqiya is an animal-driven chain of pots geared through a right-angle drive. Hama's norias are up to 20 metres in diameter.

**Why it matters.** Lifted water higher than the shaduf with no labour, making urban gardens and sugar plantations possible across the caliphate.

## Fatimid Egypt

<a id="ibn_al_haytham_optics"></a>
### Ibn al-Haytham's optics and the camera obscura

*Cairo - Book of Optics, c. 1011-1021 - science / method*

Ibn al-Haytham showed by experiment that vision happens because light enters the eye from objects (not rays leaving the eye), explained the camera obscura, studied refraction and lenses, and insisted on repeatable experiments.

**How it works.** Light travels in straight lines from every point of a lit object; through a small hole it projects an inverted image on a surface. He tested this with lamps and screens, and used the geometry to describe how the eye forms images.

**Why it matters.** The method, not just the result: he is often called the first experimental physicist, and Kepler and Newton built on the Latin Optics.

**In the games.** Mirage's Baghdad is a century and a half earlier, but the optics of the same tradition were studied there.

## Byzantine, Islamic and Crusader Levant

<a id="trebuchet"></a>
### The counterweight trebuchet

*Nicaea (1097), Acre (1191), Lisbon, Castelnaud - traction trebuchet from China (4th century BCE) via the Avars (6th century); counterweight type by the 1180s - military*

A beam on an axle with a heavy hinged counterweight on the short arm and a sling on the long arm. The biggest threw 100-kg stones over 200 metres and could batter a wall to rubble in days.

**How it works.** The counterweight (sand or stone in a box, up to 10 tonnes) falls, whipping the long arm up; the sling trails and then releases its load when the sling's loop slips off a hook set at the chosen angle. Range is tuned by the sling length and the hook angle, and the hinged box keeps the energy release efficient.

**Why it matters.** The most powerful machine of the Middle Ages before cannon; Richard I's engines at Acre and Edward I's 'Warwolf' at Stirling (1304) decided sieges.

## Medieval Europe

<a id="heavy_plough_three_field"></a>
### The heavy plough, the horse collar and three-field rotation

*northern Europe - heavy plough spreading from the 7th century; padded horse collar by the 10th; three-field system from the 8th-9th century - agriculture*

The wheeled mouldboard plough cut and turned the heavy clay soils of the north that the Mediterranean scratch plough could not; the rigid horse collar let horses pull it at full strength; rotating winter grain, spring crop and fallow raised output by a third and spread the work through the year.

**How it works.** A coulter slices the sod, the share cuts beneath it and the mouldboard turns it over, burying weeds and draining the furrow. The collar bears on the horse's shoulders instead of its windpipe. In three-field rotation each year a third of the land lies fallow and is grazed, returning manure.

**Why it matters.** Lynn White's thesis that these three changes underlie the medieval population boom is debated, but the north's shift from the Mediterranean to the Rhine-Thames axis is real.

**In the games.** Valhalla: Saxon and Norse farms in Mercia and East Anglia.

<a id="post_mill"></a>
### The European post mill and tower mill

*England, Flanders, Normandy - post mill from the 1180s; tower mill from the 14th century - power*

A horizontal-axis windmill whose whole timber body pivots on a massive post so the miller can turn the sails into the wind. The tower mill later fixed the body and rotated only the cap.

**How it works.** Four lattice sails covered with cloth turn a windshaft; a brake wheel on the shaft drives a lantern pinion (later a wallower) on the vertical shaft that turns the runner stone. The sails are set at a slight angle, like an airscrew, with cloth reefed to suit the wind.

**Why it matters.** Opened milling to flat, dry country without streams and drove the drainage of the Low Countries; 10,000 stood in England by 1300.

## Medieval Europe (with Chinese and Islamic water-clock precursors)

<a id="mechanical_clock"></a>
### The mechanical clock and the verge escapement

*Italy, England (Salisbury 1386, Wells 1392), France - weight-driven escapement clocks from the 1280s-1300s - timekeeping*

A falling weight turns a gear train; the verge-and-foliot escapement lets the train advance one tooth at a time, converting the weight's steady pull into counted beats. Su Song's Chinese water-driven astronomical clock (1092) had an escapement too, but water-driven.

**How it works.** A crown wheel with saw teeth is checked by two pallets on a vertical rod (the verge); a horizontal bar with adjustable weights (the foliot) swings back and forth, each swing releasing one tooth. Accuracy was poor (15 minutes a day) until the pendulum (1656), but the hour was now mechanical and public, struck on a bell.

**Why it matters.** Clocks on town belfries changed work discipline, and the clockmaker's gearing led to the lathe, the planetarium and precision instruments.

**In the games.** AC II: Italian towns' campanili and Leonardo's gear studies; Syndicate: Big Ben is the end of the tradition.

**In Minecraft.** `/function ac:london/westminster` (landmark); `/function ac:tech/clock_tower` (model)

**Sources.** Landes, Revolution in Time (1983); Dohrn-van Rossum, History of the Hour (1996)

## Medieval France and Europe

<a id="gothic_engineering"></a>
### Gothic engineering: pointed arch, rib vault, flying buttress

*Saint-Denis (1144), Notre-Dame de Paris (1163-1250), Chartres, Beauvais - 12th-13th centuries - construction*

The pointed arch pushes less outward than a round one and can be made any height for any span; diagonal ribs concentrate a vault's load onto piers; flying buttresses carry the remaining thrust over the aisle roofs to outer piers, so walls can be mostly glass.

**How it works.** Masons set out the geometry with compasses and templates (no structural theory); each element was proportioned by rule of thumb and by what had stood. Beauvais' choir (48 m) collapsed in 1284 at the limit. Stained glass was coloured in the melt with metal oxides and held in lead cames.

**Why it matters.** The tallest interiors before steel, and a transmission of engineering through lodge drawings (Villard de Honnecourt's sketchbook) rather than texts.

**In the games.** Unity: Notre-Dame was modelled block by block for the game; Syndicate's Westminster is Gothic Revival.

**In Minecraft.** `/function ac:paris/notre_dame` (landmark); `/function ac:tech/flying_buttress` (model)

**Sources.** Mark, Experiments in Gothic Structure (1982); Fitchen, The Construction of Gothic Cathedrals (1961)

## Norman and Crusader Europe

<a id="castles"></a>
### From motte-and-bailey to the concentric castle

*Normandy, England (1066 onward), Crusader states (Krak des Chevaliers), Wales (Beaumaris 1295) - 11th-13th centuries - military architecture*

The Normans threw up earth mounds (mottes) topped by timber towers inside a palisaded yard (bailey) in weeks; stone keeps followed, then curtain walls with round towers, and finally concentric rings of walls each overlooking the next, learned partly from Byzantine and Islamic fortresses in the Levant.

**How it works.** Height gives the defender range and the attacker nothing; round towers have no corners for mining and no blind spots. Arrow slits, machicolations (holes in the parapet floor), murder holes over gates and a moat multiply the garrison's effect. Masyaf, Alamut and Krak all sit on crags that remove the need for a moat.

**Why it matters.** A castle let a few dozen men hold a region; gunpowder cannon made its high walls a liability by 1500 and the low star fort replaced it.

**In the games.** Assassin's Creed (2007): Masyaf and Acre; Mirage: Alamut; Syndicate: the Tower of London; Hexe's Burg.

**In Minecraft.** `/function ac:holy_land/masyaf` (landmark); `/function ac:tech/motte_and_bailey` (model)

**Sources.** Kenyon, Medieval Fortifications (1990); Kennedy, Crusader Castles (1994)

## Scandinavia

<a id="longship"></a>
### Viking longships and navigation

*Norway, Denmark; Gokstad (c. 890), Oseberg (820), Skuldelev wrecks - 8th-11th centuries - naval / navigation*

Clinker-built (overlapping planks riveted together) oak hulls, light, flexible and shallow enough to row up rivers and beach anywhere, with a single square sail. Navigation used the sun, coastal landmarks, bird flight, and possibly a sun-compass and calcite 'sunstone'.

**How it works.** Planks were split radially from the log, not sawn, so the grain ran unbroken; they were lashed or trenailed to ribs added after the shell. A side rudder (steerboard, hence starboard) steered. The Gokstad replica crossed the Atlantic in 1893 at up to 11 knots.

**Why it matters.** Enabled raids from Lindisfarne (793) to Constantinople, trade to Baghdad along the Volga, and settlement of Iceland, Greenland and Vinland.

**In the games.** Valhalla: Eivor's longship and the raid mechanic; Vinland.

**In Minecraft.** `/function ac:viking/ravensthorpe` (landmark)

**Sources.** Brogger & Shetelig, The Viking Ships (1951); Crumlin-Pedersen, Viking-Age Ships and Shipbuilding in Hedeby/Haithabu and Schleswig (1997)

## Renaissance Florence

<a id="brunelleschi_dome"></a>
### Brunelleschi's dome

*Santa Maria del Fiore, Florence - 1420-1436 - construction*

A 45-metre octagonal dome built without centering (no timber forest could span it): a double shell with eight main ribs, herringbone brickwork that locked each course as it was laid, and horizontal stone and iron chains to contain the thrust. Brunelleschi also invented the ox-driven reversible hoist and a crane for the lantern.

**How it works.** Bricks laid in a herringbone spiral act as a self-supporting corbel at every stage, so the masons stood on the dome they were building. The inner shell carries load; the outer sheds rain. Sandstone chains clamped with iron and a wooden chain at the base resist hoop tension. The hoist's gearing let one ox team raise or lower loads by reversing a clutch rather than turning the animals around.

**Why it matters.** The first great engineering feat of the Renaissance and the first project to rely on a single designer's machines and geometry; Leonardo later sketched Brunelleschi's hoists.

**In the games.** AC II: Ezio climbs the Duomo; Leonardo da Vinci's workshop is next door.

**In Minecraft.** `/function ac:italy/florence` (landmark)

**Sources.** King, Brunelleschi's Dome (2000); Saalman, Filippo Brunelleschi: The Cupola of Santa Maria del Fiore (1980)

## Republic of Venice

<a id="venetian_arsenal"></a>
### The Venetian Arsenal and the lateen galley

*Venice - founded 1104; peak 15th-16th centuries - manufacturing / naval*

A 45-hectare state shipyard that at its height employed 16,000 and could fit out a galley in a day by moving the hull past stations that added rigging, oars, arms and stores: an assembly line centuries before Ford.

**How it works.** Standardised parts (interchangeable rudders, masts, benches) were stockpiled; frame-first construction from drawn moulds replaced shell-first; the hull was floated along a canal past warehouses that each issued one category of kit. The lateen (triangular) sail let galleys and later carracks sail closer to the wind.

**Why it matters.** Shows premodern mass production under state control; Galileo studied motion and strength of materials here.

**In the games.** AC II: the Arsenal is the Venice chapter's fortress; Black Flag's ships carry the lateen mizzen it popularised.

## Inca

<a id="inca_bridge_and_roads"></a>
### Inca suspension bridges and the Qhapaq Nan

*Andes, from Quito to Santiago; Q'eswachaka bridge over the Apurimac - 15th-16th centuries; the Q'eswachaka is rewoven every year to this day - transport*

A 40,000-km road network crossed the Andes with stairways, causeways and grass-rope suspension bridges spanning 45 metres and more, maintained by labour tax (mita) and served by relay runners (chasquis) and storehouses (qollqa).

**How it works.** Villagers twist ichu grass into cords, cords into cables as thick as a body, and stretch four floor cables and two handrail cables between stone anchors; a mat of branches makes the deck. The bridge sags by design and lasts about a year.

**Why it matters.** Engineering without wheels, iron or writing (records were kept on knotted quipu cords) that held together an empire of 10 million.

**In the games.** Not an AC setting yet, but Black Flag's Caribbean was the sea road to Spanish Peru's silver.

**Sources.** Hyslop, The Inka Road System (1984); Ochsendorf, 'Engineering in the Andes mountains: history and design of Inca suspension bridges', in Tall Buildings and Engineering (2006)

## Inca and earlier Andean cultures

<a id="andean_terraces"></a>
### Andean terraces (andenes) and Moray

*Sacred Valley, Machu Picchu, Moray - pre-Inca to 1530s - agriculture / earthworks*

Stepped stone-walled terraces turned slopes into level fields with deep soil and drainage, and created micro-climates; Moray's concentric terraced bowls differ by 15 degrees C from top to bottom and may have been an agricultural laboratory.

**How it works.** Each terrace has a stone retaining wall, a base layer of rubble for drainage, gravel, then imported topsoil; stone steps (sarunas) protrude from the walls. Channels from springs irrigate from the top down; the stone stores daytime heat against frost.

**Why it matters.** Fed a mountain empire and still produce; the terraces at Machu Picchu also stabilise the ridge against landslide.

**In the games.** Shadows' Iga rice terraces are the Japanese counterpart.

## Aztec (Mexica)

<a id="chinampas"></a>
### Aztec chinampas

*Lake Texcoco and Xochimilco - 14th-16th centuries - agriculture / hydraulics*

Raised fields built in the shallow lake from layers of mud and vegetation held by wattle fences and willow roots, giving up to seven harvests a year from a few metres of width. Tenochtitlan's 200,000 people depended on them.

**How it works.** A rectangle is staked out in the lake, fenced with wattle, and filled with alternating layers of lake mud and decaying plants; willows planted at the edges root through and anchor it. Canals between the plots serve for transport and irrigation by hand; dredged canal mud renews the soil.

**Why it matters.** Among the most productive farming systems ever devised, with a dyke (Nezahualcoyotl's, 16 km) separating fresh from brackish water.

**In the games.** No AC game is set in Mesoamerica; Liberation's bayou and Black Flag's Caribbean are the nearest settings.

## Teotihuacan, Maya, Aztec

<a id="mesoamerican_pyramids"></a>
### Mesoamerican pyramids and Maya astronomy

*Teotihuacan (Pyramid of the Sun, c. 200 CE), Tikal, Chichen Itza - c. 100 BCE to 1500 CE - construction / astronomy*

Stepped platforms of rubble and adobe faced with cut stone and lime plaster, often enlarged by building a new pyramid over the old. Alignments and stairways encode calendars: El Castillo at Chichen Itza has 365 steps and casts a serpent shadow at the equinoxes.

**How it works.** No metal tools, draft animals or wheels: stone was cut with harder stone, carried by tumpline, and bonded with lime plaster burned from limestone, which consumed forests. The Maya Long Count and their eclipse tables (Dresden Codex) came from naked-eye observation over centuries.

**Why it matters.** Independent invention of monumental architecture, positional notation (with zero) and precise astronomy, isolated from the Old World.

**In the games.** Black Flag: Mayan ruins on the Caribbean islands hold the Mayan stela puzzles.

## Shona; Nok and Bantu-speaking ironworkers

<a id="great_zimbabwe"></a>
### Great Zimbabwe and African ironworking

*Zimbabwe plateau; West and Central Africa - iron smelting in West Africa by c. 500 BCE; Great Zimbabwe 11th-15th centuries - construction / metallurgy*

Great Zimbabwe's Great Enclosure is a dry-stone wall 250 metres round and 11 metres high, built of granite blocks with no mortar, enclosing a conical tower; it was the capital of a gold-trading state linked to the Swahili coast and the Indian Ocean. Sub-Saharan Africa smelted iron in natural-draught and bellows furnaces from the mid-first millennium BCE.

**How it works.** Granite exfoliates in sheets when fire-heated and quenched, giving flat blocks that were laid in coursed battered walls without mortar. African furnaces, some three metres tall with chimney draught alone, reached bloomery temperatures and produced steel-like iron.

**Why it matters.** Monumental stone architecture and an independent iron tradition that colonial writers long refused to credit to Africans.

**In the games.** Freedom Cry: Adewale's story begins with people taken from these coasts.

## Sengoku and Azuchi-Momoyama Japan

<a id="japanese_castle"></a>
### Japanese castles: ishigaki and the tenshu

*Azuchi (1579), Osaka (1583), Himeji - 1570s-1600s - military architecture*

Massive curved dry-stone bases (ishigaki) that shrug off earthquakes carry a timber-and-plaster keep (tenshu) of five or more storeys, surrounded by moats and baileys laid out as a maze to channel attackers under fire from loopholes and stone-drop windows.

**How it works.** Ishigaki walls lean inward in a fan curve; the stones are wedged with small fill and sit on a rubble bed that lets the wall flex. The tenshu is a post-and-beam frame coated with fireproof lime plaster over bamboo lath; the tiled, flared roofs shed rain and the gables (chidori hafu) are decoration. Gun loopholes (sama) answered the Portuguese arquebus that reached Japan in 1543.

**Why it matters.** Azuchi, Nobunaga's castle, was the first of the great tenshu and a political statement as much as a fortress.

**In the games.** Shadows: Azuchi Castle is the seat of Oda Nobunaga, Yasuke's lord.

**Sources.** Coaldrake, Architecture and Authority in Japan (1996); Turnbull, Japanese Castles 1540-1640 (2003)

## Japan

<a id="katana_tatara"></a>
### Tatara smelting and the katana

*Izumo and Bitchu - tatara from the 6th century; curved katana from the Kamakura period (12th century) - metallurgy*

The tatara is a low clay furnace fed with iron sand and charcoal for three days, yielding a bloom (kera) of mixed steel; the best high-carbon pieces (tamahagane) are folded and forge-welded, then a hard jacket is wrapped around a soft core and differentially hardened with a clay coat to give the curved, sharp, resilient blade.

**How it works.** Folding (about 15 times, giving 30,000 layers) homogenises carbon and drives out slag. Clay is applied thick on the spine and thin on the edge before quenching, so the edge forms hard martensite while the spine stays tough pearlite; the volume change of the martensite bends the blade into its curve and leaves the hamon line.

**Why it matters.** A composite material engineered by craft knowledge alone; the sword became the samurai's badge in the Tokugawa peace.

**In the games.** Shadows: Naoe's and Yasuke's blades.

**Sources.** Kapp, Kapp & Yoshihara, The Craft of the Japanese Sword (1987); Inoue, 'Tatara and the Japanese sword', in Bronze and Iron (1993)

## Neolithic Britain

<a id="stonehenge_and_megaliths"></a>
### Stonehenge and megalithic engineering

*Salisbury Plain - c. 3000-2000 BCE (sarsen circle c. 2500 BCE) - construction / astronomy*

Sarsen stones of up to 30 tonnes were dragged 25 km from the Marlborough Downs, shaped with stone hammers, raised into pits, and capped with lintels fixed by mortise-and-tenon and tongue-and-groove joints copied from woodworking. The bluestones came 250 km from Wales.

**How it works.** Uprights were tipped into a pit against a sloped side and hauled upright with ropes and a timber A-frame; lintels were probably raised on a rising timber crib. The axis aligns on midsummer sunrise and midwinter sunset.

**Why it matters.** Shows that a society without metal, writing or wheels could organise thousands of people and plan over generations.

**In the games.** Valhalla: Stonehenge is a standing-stone puzzle site.

**Sources.** Parker Pearson, Stonehenge: Exploring the Greatest Stone Age Mystery (2012); Pitts, How to Build Stonehenge (2022)

## Mediterranean and Atlantic (Islamic, Portuguese, Spanish, English)

<a id="sugar_mill_plantation"></a>
### The sugar mill: the first factory in the Americas

*Cyprus, Madeira, Brazil, Barbados, Jamaica, Saint-Domingue - three-roller mill from c. 1600; Caribbean sugar revolution 1640-1700 - manufacturing / agriculture*

Cane had to be crushed within hours of cutting and the juice boiled in a train of copper kettles, so the plantation combined field, mill (animal, water or wind powered) and boiling house in one industrial unit, run on the labour of enslaved Africans.

**How it works.** Three vertical iron-shod rollers geared together crush the cane twice as it passes; juice runs to the boiling house where it is limed, skimmed and boiled down through successively smaller kettles until it crystallises, then cured in clay pots. Windmills (tower mills) dominated in Barbados.

**Why it matters.** A premodern technology package (from Islamic Mediterranean sugar) industrialised into the engine of the Atlantic slave trade; its profits financed the ports of Bristol, Nantes and Liverpool.

**In the games.** Black Flag and Freedom Cry: Kingston and Port-au-Prince plantations; Adewale's war on the slavers.

**In Minecraft.** `/function ac:caribbean/kingston` (landmark)

**Sources.** Mintz, Sweetness and Power (1985); Galloway, The Sugar Cane Industry (1989)
